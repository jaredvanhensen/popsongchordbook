Lesson 1

Chord Group 1: White Key Chords (C, F, G Shapes)

Start by learning the fundamental "white key shapes" where the chords consist entirely of white keys. Once mastered, we apply the minor equivalents (Am, Dm, Em) which share identical physical shapes.

💡 Chord Shapes Theory

Major chords (C, F, G) on white keys use standard shape spacing (root, skip 3 semitones, skip 2 semitones). The minor shapes (Am, Dm, Em) have the exact same shape index, making them easy transitions.

🖐️ C Major Shape Fingering

Use right hand fingers: 1 (Thumb) on C, 3 (Middle) on E, and 5 (Pinky) on G. Apply this same physical shape to F and G major chords, as well as the minor chords (Am, Dm, Em)!

Chord Group 2: The Mountain or ∧-shape Chords (D, E, A Shapes)

Now we move to shapes containing one black key in the middle. Because the black key sits between two white keys, we refer to these physically as "triangle shapes".

💡 Chord Shapes Theory

Chords like D (D - F# - A), E (E - G# - B), and A (A - C# - E) feature a black key played by the middle finger, surrounded by white keys for the thumb and pinky finger.

Use right hand fingers: 1 (Thumb) on D, 3 (Middle) on the black key F#, and 5 (Pinky) on A. Apply this middle-finger-black-key spacing to E and A major chords!

Lesson 3

Chord Group 3: The Valley or V-shape Chords (Db, Eb, Ab)

These chords are inverted "triangles" where the thumb and pinky sit on black keys, and the middle finger sits on a white key. Mastering this provides easy access to pop songs written in flat keys.

💡 Chord Shapes Theory

Db (Db - F - Ab), Eb (Eb - G - Bb), and Ab (Ab - C - Eb) major chords are bound by black keys on the outside, and a single white key in the middle.

🎹 Practice Chord Spacing (Hover/Click to visualize)

Db Major Eb Major Ab Major
🖐️ Db Major Shape Fingering

Use right hand fingers: 1 (Thumb) on the black key Db, 3 (Middle) on the white key F, and 5 (Pinky) on the black key Ab. Apply this same black-white-black shape to Eb and Ab major chords!

Chord Group 4: The Outliers (B & Bb Chords)

The B and Bb chords don't fit perfectly in standard symmetric shapes. They are transitional shapes that require distinct placements.

💡 Chord Shapes Theory

Bb major (Bb - D - F) begins with a black key followed by two white keys. B major (B - D# - F#) starts with a white key followed by two black keys.

🎹 Practice Chord Spacing (Hover/Click to visualize)

🖐️ B Major & Bb Major Fingering
B Major (White-Black-Black)
Bb Major (Black-White-White)

For B Major: Right hand fingers 1 on B, 3 on D#, 5 on F#. For Bb Major: Right hand fingers 1 on Bb, 3 on D, 5 on F.

Chord Group 5: The 3 Black Key Chords (F# / Gb and D#m / Ebm)

F# Major (F# - A# - C#) and D# Minor (D# - F# - A#) are the only triads consisting entirely of black keys. Learning these completes your circle of basic triads!

💡 Chord Shapes Theory

To play F#, place your thumb on F#, middle finger on A#, and pinky on C#. For D#m / Ebm, play D#, F#, and A#. All notes sit in the 3-black-key cluster!

Lesson 6

Adding the Bass Note: Playing with Two Hands

This is where the magic happens. To make your music sound rich, professional, and deep, you must use your left hand effectively.

⭐ The Golden Rule of Bass

Your Left Hand should ALWAYS play the Root Note (the BASS note) of the chord.

If you are playing a C Major chord with your right hand, play a low C (or two C's an octave apart) with your left hand. This "anchors" the sound and gives it that professional piano-ballad feel.

🖐️ Two Hand Technique
Left Hand Plays Root Note (Low Bass)
Right Hand Plays the Triad or Inversion

Lesson 7

Group 7: Chord Patterns & Arpeggios

Learn how to break apart block chords into rhythmic patterns and fluid arpeggios that bring pop songs to life.

⭐ Why Play Patterns & Arpeggios?

Playing block chords (striking all notes together) works well, but doing it continuously can sound robotic, heavy, or static.

Arpeggios (playing chord notes one-by-one) and rhythmic patterns create motion, elegance, and emotional depth. They allow you to build intensity from a gentle verse to a powerful chorus!

💡 Understanding Note Numbers (1, 3, 5, 8, 10)

1 (Root Note) 1st note of scale/chord (e.g. C). Sets the chord foundation.
3 (Third) 3rd note of scale (e.g. E). Determines Major vs Minor.
5 (Fifth) 5th note of scale (e.g. G). Adds harmonic stability.
8 (Octave) 8th note (same as Root 1, but 1 octave higher, e.g. High C).
10 (Tenth) 10th note (same as Third 3, but 1 octave higher, e.g. High E). Creates wide pop voicing.

🎵 6 Essential Pop Chord Patterns

1️⃣
The Broken Triad (1 – 3 – 5 – 3)

Play the 3 notes of the chord one-by-one in a steady rocking motion. LH plays Root Bass; RH plays 1 – 3 – 5 – 3.

LH Bass (C) → RH C → E → G → E
2️⃣
Ascending Triad Roll (1 – 3 – 5 – 1 – 3 – 5 – 1 – 3)

A driving 8th-note ascending roll using fingers 1, 3, 5 over LH Root Bass. Creates forward momentum in energetic pop tracks.

LH Bass (C) → RH C → E → G → C → E → G → C → E
3️⃣
Coldplay - Clocks Pattern (5 – 3 – 1 – 5 – 3 – 1 – 5 – 3)

The legendary piano riff from Coldplay's 'Clocks' in Eb Major. LH holds the Root Bass (Eb); RH plays descending 5 – 3 – 1 twice (Eb → Bb → G) and ends on 5 – 3. This is the same as pattern two but now the other way around.

LH Bass (Eb) → RH Eb → Bb → G → Eb → Bb → G → Eb → Bb
4️⃣
Pulsing Bass-Chord Split

Left hand strikes the low Bass on Beat 1 & 3; Right hand pulses light chords on Beats 2 & 4.

LH Bass (C) → RH Chord (C-E-G)
5️⃣
Waterfall Arpeggio (Bass – 1 – 3 – 5 – 8)

Start with low Left Hand bass, then roll smoothly upwards through the right hand notes up to the octave.

LH Low C → RH C → E → G → High C
6️⃣
1 – 5 – 8 – 10 Modern Pop Roll

Spanned open 4-note voicing (Adele / Coldplay style). LH plays 1 + 5 (C + G), RH plays 8 + 10 (C + E) letting all 4 notes ring out together.

LH 1+5 (C + G) → RH 8+10 (High C + E)

Lesson 8

Lesson 8: Chord Inversions

Learn how to rearrange the notes of a chord into different orders — called inversions. This unlocks smoother hand movement, richer piano sounds, and gives you the tools to understand chord notation used in sheet music and apps.

⭐ What Is a Chord Inversion?

A chord inversion is simply playing the same 3 notes of a chord in a different order — starting from a note other than the root.

Instead of always starting on C (C – E – G), you can start on G (G – C – E) or E (E – G – C). The chord still sounds like C Major — just with a different color and bass note. This makes moving between chords much smoother because your hand doesn't have to jump as far.

📖 How This App Notates Inversions: C2, C3 & Slash Notation

In this app, inversions are written with a number after the chord name to show which position the root note sits in:

Notation Name Note Order Meaning
C Root Position C – E – G C is in 1st position. Standard triad.
C3 1st Inversion E – G – C C is in 3rd position. E is lowest note.
C2 2nd Inversion G – C – E C is in 2nd position. G is lowest note.
C/E Slash Notation E – G – C C chord with E as the lowest (bass) note. Same as C3.
C/G Slash Notation G – C – E C chord with G as the lowest (bass) note. Same as C2.

🎹 The 3 Positions of C Major

1️⃣
Root Position — C

C is in 1st position

C3 (1) + E3 (3) + G3 (5)
C – E – G

Fingers: 1 – 3 – 5. The standard, most common chord position.

2️⃣
1st Inversion — C3

C is in 3rd position · also written as C/E

E3 (1) + G3 (2) + C4 (5)
E – G – C

Fingers: 1 – 2 – 5. E is the lowest note. C sits in position 3.

3️⃣
2nd Inversion — C2

C is in 2nd position · also written as C/G

G3 (1) + C4 (2) + E4 (5)
G – C – E

Fingers: 1 – 2 – 5. G is the lowest note. C sits in position 2.

🎯 Pro Tip: When moving from Am → C, instead of jumping to Root Position C (C–E–G), use 1st Inversion C2 (G–C–E). Your pinky barely moves! Inversions are the secret to smooth, professional-sounding transitions.
